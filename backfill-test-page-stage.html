The Drifters of Vessar: Life on a World Built From Silicon

On a planet where stone breathes and the sky is full of thinking creatures, gravity is not a law to obey but a language to speak. A visit to the strangest neighbors across the veil.

By Mara Olenko  ·  Published October 28, 2026  ·  6 min read

The first thing visitors notice about Vessar is the sound. It is not quite music and not quite wind, but a low, glassy chiming that rolls across the plains at dusk, when the temperature drops and millions of crystalline organisms contract at once. The locals, if you can call them that, do not hear it the way we do. They feel it as a ripple in the gravitational field around them, a nightly tide that tells them the day is ending and the long cold is on its way.

Vessar sits in a parallel universe whose physics are close enough to ours to be familiar and different enough to be unsettling. Its sun is a fat orange star, its year runs a little over four hundred of our days, and its surface would kill an unprotected human in minutes. But it is alive, extravagantly so, and nearly everything alive there is built not on carbon but on silicon.

A Chemistry Written in Stone

On Earth, silicon is the stuff of sand, quartz and computer chips. It sits directly beneath carbon on the periodic table and shares its talent for forming four bonds, which is why science fiction writers have been daydreaming about silicon life for more than a century. In our universe, the idea runs into a stubborn problem: silicon dioxide, the equivalent of the carbon dioxide we exhale, is a solid. A silicon creature on Earth would essentially breathe out gravel.

Vessar solves these problems with a hotter, denser atmosphere rich in hydrogen and fluorine compounds, and with an ocean that is not water at all but a slow-moving sea of liquid methane silanes. In that chemical bath, long silicon chains known to researchers as silanes and silicones fold and link in ways they never could at home. The result is a biosphere that looks, at first glance, like a mineral collection that has learned to move.

The plains are carpeted with what visitors call glassgrass, thin translucent spines that absorb sunlight and store it as electrical charge rather than sugar. Larger structures, the reefs, grow along the shorelines of the silane seas, layered like geodes and rising hundreds of meters into the sky. When a reef dies, it does not rot. It simply stops growing and becomes a cliff, and over millions of years the continents of Vessar have been built almost entirely from the bodies of the dead.

Meet the Drifters

The most remarkable inhabitants of Vessar, and the reason so many researchers have risked the crossing, are the beings our survey teams named the Drifters. They call themselves something closer to a long, falling tone, which our instruments render as a dip in local gravity followed by a slow recovery.

A mature Drifter is roughly the size of a hot-air balloon and shaped something like a jellyfish carved from smoked glass. Its body is a lattice of silicon-oxygen frameworks threaded with metallic veins, and at its core sits a dense, slowly rotating mass that researchers believe is the organ responsible for its most astonishing ability. Drifters do not fly. They do not swim through the air on wings or gas bladders. They float because they have learned to bend gravity around themselves.

"We kept looking for the trick. A lifting gas, a magnetic field, anything. It took us two years to accept that the trick was the thing itself. They simply tell the planet to let go of them." — Dr. Ilse Varga, field xenobiologist

Exactly how they do it remains the central mystery of Vessarian science. The leading theory holds that the spinning core, packed with exotic crystalline structures that only form under Vessar's peculiar physics, can locally reshape the gravitational field in a bubble a few dozen meters wide. Inside that bubble, a Drifter can reduce its effective weight to almost nothing, push itself sideways by tilting the field, or drop like a stone by letting the bubble collapse.

Speaking in Weight

Because their entire existence is organized around gravity, the Drifters appear to communicate through it as well. Survey instruments placed among the herds pick up constant, minute fluctuations in local gravitational pull, patterned and repeating and far too structured to be accidental. Linguists working on the problem believe that what we think of as words, Drifters express as shifts in weight: a sudden heaviness, a lightening, a tilt to the left.

That has made conversation, in any meaningful sense, extremely difficult. Human researchers cannot produce gravitational signals without heavy equipment, and the first attempts to do so ended badly when a prototype transmitter accidentally broadcast something that sent an entire herd fleeing across the sea. Today, contact teams use slow, deliberate pulses and a growing vocabulary of a few hundred agreed-upon signals. The Drifters, for their part, seem patient.

What has been learned so far suggests a society organized around memory rather than territory. Drifters migrate constantly, following the seasonal shift of sunlight across the plains, and they appear to carry detailed maps of the planet stored as patterns in their crystalline bodies. Elders, who can live for many centuries, are larger and heavier and seem to anchor the herd. When an elder dies, the herd circles its body for days, and younger Drifters press close to it, possibly absorbing fragments of the lattice and, with them, fragments of what the elder knew.

Daily Life Among the Clouds

A typical day for a Drifter begins at the warm edge of the terminator, where the slow Vessarian dawn spills across the plains. The herd rises to bask, spreading their translucent bodies wide to soak up light and charge their internal batteries. By midday they descend to graze on glassgrass, drawing minerals up through fine filaments that trail beneath them like the roots of an upturned plant.

Young Drifters spend much of their time practicing what researchers call falling games, repeatedly collapsing and restoring their gravity bubbles to see how close to the ground they can drop before catching themselves. At night, as the plains chime and contract, the herd drifts higher to escape the cold that settles in the valleys, hanging in the dark like a constellation of dim lanterns.

Why Vessar Matters

It would be easy to treat Vessar as a curiosity, a cosmic oddity on the far side of a door most people will never pass through. But the researchers who work there argue that it is far more important than that. For generations, our ideas about life have been shaped by a sample size of one. Vessar breaks that pattern completely.

It proves that intelligence does not require carbon, water or even wings and legs. It suggests that the forces we consider fixed may, under the right conditions, become tools in the hands of something alive. And it raises an uncomfortable question for anyone who has ever looked up at our own night sky and assumed we would know life when we saw it. On Vessar, the first survey team spent three weeks mapping what they believed were weather balloons of unknown origin before one of them politely lowered itself to eye level and waited.

The Drifters are still waiting, in their way. Each season, more researchers make the crossing, carrying better instruments and a slightly larger vocabulary of careful, weighted words. Progress is slow. But somewhere over the chiming plains, a herd of glass giants is learning to speak with us, one gentle shift of gravity at a time.
