How to Plan a Family Reunion

Organizing a reunion for the first time is equal parts logistics and storytelling. Here's how to bring relatives together and make the day feel meaningful for everyone.

By Anna Sutherland  ·  Published May 2, 2026  ·  5 min read

There's something uniquely satisfying about a well-organized family reunion. Not the chaotic kind where nobody knows where to be or what to bring, but the kind where cousins who haven't seen each other in fifteen years are laughing over the same picnic table, and somebody has thought to print out an old group photo from the 1970s and pass it around. Planning one is a bigger project than most people expect, but the work itself is surprisingly fun — especially if you approach it as part event coordination, part family storytelling.

Start With the Guest List

The first real task is figuring out who you actually want to invite. For a close-extended reunion — siblings, their kids, a grandparent or two — the list builds itself. But for anything larger, you'll quickly run into a question most families rarely stop to examine: who counts as family? Second cousins? Great-aunts you've met twice? The partners of everyone involved? There's no universal answer, but deciding early gives you a defensible guest count, which drives everything else: venue, budget, food.

This is also where many first-time organizers discover gaps in the family network. Somebody mentions an uncle who moved to Arizona in the 1980s and nobody's spoken to in years. A cousin married into the family but their side is a complete unknown. If you want to expand the circle, a little family history research at this stage pays off — old address books, a pass through genealogy records to confirm surnames and birth years, and a few phone calls to the oldest relatives who might have contact information. It's common for a reunion to become the event that puts a family back in touch with branches that drifted apart.

Pick a Date, Pick a Place

Summer weekends remain the default for good reason — kids are out of school, the weather cooperates in most regions, and people are more willing to travel. But summer is also crowded with weddings and vacations, so send out a save-the-date at least four to six months in advance. A simple online poll can narrow down two or three candidate weekends before you commit.

For location, the two main options are a family home (if someone has the space and the nerve) or a neutral venue. State parks with reserved pavilions are wildly underrated — they're cheap, they handle the parking issue, and they come with bathrooms and picnic tables built in. Community centers, church halls, and small event spaces work well for larger groups. If your family is geographically scattered, picking a location that's a reasonable flight or drive for most attendees will boost turnout far more than picking the "ancestral hometown" that nobody actually lives near anymore.

Make the Day About More Than Lunch

A reunion that's just a meal works for small groups, but anything over fifteen or twenty people benefits from some structure. Not a rigid schedule — nobody wants that — but a few anchor activities that give the day shape and encourage people to mix beyond their immediate branch of the family.

A few ideas that consistently work well: a printed family tree displayed on an easel so people can find themselves and their connections; a photo board with old family images labeled by decade; a "find someone who…" icebreaker for the kids and teens; a shared storytelling hour where the oldest attendees share memories while someone records audio. If you want a more unusual anchor, consider coordinating AncestryDNA kits among a handful of cousins in the months before the reunion. Comparing ethnicity estimates and shared DNA matches at the reunion itself sparks real conversation — it's one of the few group activities that can engage a teenager and an eighty-year-old with equal curiosity, and it often surfaces branches of the family nobody knew to ask about.

"The best reunions feel like a low-key celebration of the fact that all these people, somehow, belong to the same story."

Food, Logistics, and the Small Stuff

Potluck is the path of least resistance and usually the best choice. Assign rough categories — mains, sides, desserts, drinks — so you don't end up with fourteen pasta salads. For larger reunions or multi-day events, a simple catered option for one meal takes enormous pressure off the host family. Build in a 15 to 20 percent buffer in your headcount for food, because somebody always brings an extra kid or a plus-one.

Small touches go a long way. Name tags with surname and branch (e.g., "Maria — James's side") help distant relatives place each other. A simple printed handout listing the family tree, key dates, and a short history of how the family came to this country or this region is often kept and treasured long after the reunion ends. If your family has any documented history at all — an immigration date, a military service record, a story about the town everyone originally came from — working those details into the day gives the whole event a through-line beyond the burgers and lawn chairs.

After the Reunion

The last step is the one most organizers skip, but it's the one that makes the next reunion easier. Within a week or two, send a group message thanking everyone, share a few favorite photos from the day, and ask anyone interested to contribute to a shared family tree or photo archive. Momentum fades quickly after an event, but a follow-up note keeps the connection warm.

Plenty of families end up deciding, right there at the picnic table, that they want to do this every two or three years. That's a good sign. The goal of a reunion isn't just the single gathering — it's the tradition of showing up for each other across distance and time. When it works, a reunion becomes the thing your family organizes around. And that's worth the spreadsheet.
